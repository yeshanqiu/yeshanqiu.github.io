Research informs design

From research guidelines and prediction to the design studio

The first studies build two things: a set of guidelines for how urban form can support birds, and an ability to predict how a change in form would shift bird diversity. Both are only useful if they can enter the place where decisions about form are actually made. This last piece is an early attempt to do exactly that, inside a design studio.

Studio
Joint design studio, National University of Singapore and Tsinghua University
Led by
A/P Hwang Yun Hye and Prof. Tan Puay Yok (NUS), A/P Zhang Ye (Tsinghua)
Partner institution
A/P Huang Jianxiang, HKU Urban Planning
My contribution
Biodiversity guidelines drawn from the research, and the bird species richness prediction model

In a joint design studio between the National University of Singapore and Tsinghua University, led by A/P Hwang Yun Hye and Prof. Tan Puay Yok (NUS) and A/P Zhang Ye (Tsinghua), with partner institution A/P Huang Jianxiang (HKU Urban Planning), we worked on real sites in Hong Kong. Each site is a one-kilometre grid with its own mix of woodland, water, housing, and industry, from woodland edges and mid-rise housing to riverfronts, brownfield, and industrial new towns.

Our team built a decision dashboard that predicts how a site would perform across several urban ecosystem functions at once, including flood mitigation, land surface temperature, public health, local economy, and biodiversity. Students could adjust the two- and three-dimensional form of a site and read the predicted change it would bring, including the change in bird species richness.

On the biodiversity side, I did two things: I translated the research findings into a set of design guidelines for bird-friendly urban form, and I trained the model that turns a change in form into a predicted change in bird species richness.

What happened next was interesting, and not simple. Students began to reason about design from an ecological angle they might not have reached on their own, testing how a shift in massing or green space would move the numbers.

What the experiment showed

The experiment also showed its limits, and each limit pointed somewhere. A predicted number can narrow imagination as easily as it can open it, so a useful tool has to offer direction and trade-offs rather than a single answer. The questions students asked, why a change helped or hurt birds, showed that prediction alone is not enough without the processes behind it. And a real Hong Kong site is far messier than a standardized grid, which asks for models that can handle specific, complex places.

We make no claim that the panel produced better designs. It was an exploratory experiment in how research findings might enter a design process. What it taught me is concrete: to inform design well, a model needs to be more mature, more process-aware, and able to speak to real sites. That is the work I am building toward next.
